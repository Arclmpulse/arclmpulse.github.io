Well, I can't exactly say Bethesda are particularly creative with their plots...

Yes, you are looking for a family member again in this one after you exit Vault 111. This time, your son. I found the opening narrative to be very weak, if it wasn't obvious already. The game starts to open up once you leave the suburb of Sanctuary and you begin to trudge into Cambridge/Lexington, where the Brotherhood of Steel are trying to make a foothold in the area. And once you arrive to Downtown Boston proper, I think the story begins to take off from there once you clue in that the Institute has your son... but the problem is, how do you find an urban legend?

Four major factions in the game. East Coast **Brotherhood** return from FO3, not as the peacekeepers of DC like they were in that game, but rather a more classical "return to roots" technology hoarders they are in the other games as we find out that Sarah Lyons from FO3 died (I have my own Assassination conspiracy theory on that) and the great grandson of the founder of the BoS, Arthur Maxson is all grown up and the leader of this small army. Then we have the **Minutemen**, which are the last remains of "normal" people in the Boston area who are trying to create a network of towns and recreate a normal, thriving Boston again. **The Institute** (AKA MIT who went underground before the nukes fell) who have your son and are beginning to run some diabolical experiments and "playing god" with creating synthetic humans and creating a surveillance state. And lastly, the **Railroad**, who want to put an end to the Institute and give these synths a second chance at life.

While there are four endings like in New Vegas and 4 major factions, I actually would argue there is a main antagonist in this game as 3 of the endings lead to you dealing with him at the end - your son, Shaun. Also known as Father, and the leader of the Institute funnily enough. I think it was a twist that most people probably saw coming, but indeed, Kellogg kidnapped your son nearly 60 years in the past in that brief cutscene in the Vault where he unfroze everyone, then refroze you. And now he wants to incorporate synths on the surface to try and rebuild Boston while clearing out the raiders and mutants infesting it... under the Institute's watching eyes and helpful hands. So, a surveilllance state. I think he was a very interesting character and honestly proof of Nature vs Nurture - Shaun grew up to become a very morally ambiguous man with good intentions, which is really the whole philosophy of the Institute.

And as the villain of the first act of the game before you find a way to get into the Institute, I think Conrad Kellogg was one of the better villains Bethesda's cooked up, if only because of how good of a parallel he was to your player character. Kellogg basically represented everything that the Sole Survivor should've become after losing his wife and daughter. Both were people out of time, with the Sole Survivor being cryogenically frozen and Kellogg becoming a cyborg, and both lost their families. Though I suppose the difference is, Kellogg wasn't ever truly able to move on from the death of his family for nearly a century, while you, the player character, haven't had the time to become as jaded as he has with the world. I just think its a shame and a missed opportunity that you can't talk him out of the fight in Fort Hagen.

The gameplay/combat loop in FO4 was incredible, honestly. It was one of the more fun looter-shooter type games I've played, and VATS being changed to be more of a bullet time sim instead of freezing time made way more logical sense to me, at least as a in-universe explanation. I wasn't huge on them removing traits and skills, so the levelling system was more of a flop for me. And of course I think everyone knows the stupid dialogue wheel at this point, I have no clue what Bethesda were thinking with that when Fallout is all about the jargon and asking people a bunch of random, useless questions and passsing/failing skill and speech checks. I don't think it helped that the protagonist was voiced this time, which in itself was quite controversial. But still, didn't really agree with a lot of the gameplay elements they changed.

There were a lot of interesting companions, though I think overall they were a step down from FNV's. Nick Valentine is one of the most interesting characters in the series as an early prototype synth that only remembers his "human" memories but can't remember his time in the Institute. I think Cait and Deacon were quite interesting as well, the former being an addict who ultimately wants to change herself, and the latter because you never quite know if he's really being straight with you or not. And there's an interesting theory that Deacon is the Lone Wanderer from FO3, but I don't really buy into it.

The sidequests... well, they were a step down from FO3/FNV's content. There were a few sidequests that were genuinely brilliant - Silver Shroud was one, USS Consistution was another. Confidence Man was really fun too, and I think everyone loves Nick Valentine's companion quest. But the game fell into the trap of uninteresting fetch quests and leaned heavily on Preston Garvey giving you infinitely radiant missions, which was horrible.

As for the DLCs, Automatron was short, but it was actually kinda fun. The final fight with the waves of robots in the factory was one of the more unique missions in Fallout, and definitely one of the hardest fights in the series. Far Harbour is glazed by the fans of the series, and for good reason. It takes you up to an island of the coast of Maine, where Nick Valentine's "synth brother" from the Institute is harbouring a safe haven for synths on the island... but is hiding some very dark and disturbing secrets for the three factions that coexist uneasily on the island. Deciding whether to execute DiMA who's been controlling all three factions like a puppet master, letting him be to his own devices and keep the illusion of peace going, or going halfway and letting the people decide what to do with him was one of the most complex moral decisions Bethesda's posed, ever. I actually think going along with DiMA's plan to replace the Children of Atom's leader/warmonger with a docile guy and leaving Far Harbour in the dark that their leader got replaced by a synth long ago is the best path for securing peace on the island, but is it really worth giving an unreliable robot that much control?

And, well, Nuka World was one of the DLCs of all time. If you turned your brain off it was kinda fun to reclaim the amusment park, but don't go looking for too much meaning here.

This game was really fun if you honestly ignored the main story. Build up your settlements, run around in power armor and feel like a weird fusion of Hulk and Iron Man. Kill, Loot, Repeat. But it really did dumb down the series in a way that felt like a bit of a betrayal to me. It's that classic line. FO4 was objectively a great game... but it wasn't a great Fallout game. And I do agree, though I think some fans play down how fun of a game it really was. I think it's worth a play, but you'll be disappointed if you're coming from an older Fallout, for sure.
